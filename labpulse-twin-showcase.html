How it is built

Three tiers, and each one
exists for a different reason.

This is the part that makes the twin believable rather than a nice animation. We validate the engine on hardware we can touch, then apply the same engine to instruments we cannot.

Tier 1

Twin Verified

real machine + digital twin

An instrumented motor on the bench, streaming live. We predict, then induce the fault physically and check whether reality lands where the model said it would.

Its job is proof. Without it, everything else is a simulation nobody has to believe.
Tier 2

Twin Sim

digital only, any instrument

Full 3D models of HPLC systems, spectrometers and other instruments. Nobody will let us induce a fault on a working analyser, and nobody can put one on a demo table.

Its job is reach. The same engine, on instruments we will never physically hold.
Tier 3

Twin Lab

experiment before you commit

A sandbox where a lab tests a change first: a software update, a heavier schedule, a new method, a long campaign. Every session ends in a verdict and a report, not a shrug.

Its job is value. This is the tier a customer pays for.
The engine is validated on hardware we can touch, then applied to instruments we cannot. That order matters.
What you can actually ask it

Four real decisions,
made badly today.

A sandbox with no question is a toy. So the lab is built around the decisions labs already make on instinct, each one ending in an answer you can file.

01

Can I take this vendor update?

A third-party add-on ships a new version. Today you either install it and hope, or freeze the version for a year. Both are bad answers.

Replay the new version against archived runs
→ per-instrument verdict, before it touches hardware
02

Will this run actually finish?

A 96 hour panel with 312 patient samples is about to start. The instrument looks fine. Looking fine is not the same as surviving four days under load.

Completion probability, dominant risk component
→ service first, or start with confidence
03

What does this method change cost me?

Higher flow rate, harsher buffer, longer gradient. Everyone knows it wears the instrument faster. Nobody knows by how much until the parts start failing.

Column, seal and lamp life consumed per method
→ the true cost of a method, before you adopt it
04

Can one instrument carry this campaign?

Eight weeks of work, one machine or two. Today this is decided by whoever sounds most confident in the meeting.

Both plans projected side by side
→ failure dates, total cost, risk of missing the deadline
Built and working

Version Compatibility Sandbox

This one came straight out of the second evaluation. An instrument runs a third-party add-on module. When that vendor ships an update, results can shift in ways nobody catches until the data is already out. Validating it properly means re-running standards on every instrument: days of machine time, columns and reference material spent. So most labs skip it, or freeze the version and fall behind.

01
Take the lab's archived runs from the approved version — the golden set. It already exists, sitting in their records.
02
Replay the candidate version against those same inputs, entirely in software. No instrument time, no columns, no sample.
03
Compare every metric that matters — retention time, peak area, resolution, tailing, signal to noise, integration boundaries, classification agreement — against the lab's own acceptance limits.
04
Return a verdict per instrument, not per version. Compatibility depends on configuration, so the same update can be safe on one machine and wrong on the next.
05
Pin the failing instruments to the previous version, and generate the evidence to send the vendor — every failing metric against its limit.

The failures are not random. All three share one configuration — the new baseline detection opens the integration window early on noisier detectors, absorbing part of the neighbouring peak. Seven instruments take the update. Three stay pinned, with evidence.
Days of instrument time, replaced by a replay that costs nothing. And a rollback you can justify in writing.

Why this is a product and not a feature

The same engine sells
to both sides of the room.

An instrument maker does not primarily earn from the box. The durable revenue is service contracts and consumables. A twin serves both at once, which is why it works as a subscription add-on rather than a line item.

Value one

It forecasts consumables before they are consumed

Column, seal and lamp life are not spent by time, they are spent by workload. Tell the twin the plan and it tells you the parts. The lab stops running out mid campaign, and the manufacturer gets a demand signal per instrument instead of a reorder call.

Value two

It protects the sample, not just the machine

On a multi-day immunology panel the downtime cost is almost irrelevant next to what is loaded inside. Patient samples cannot be re-collected. The real question is whether the run finishes — and that is a question worth paying to answer.

Value three

It defends the service contract

Renewal stops being an argument about visit counts. It becomes a document: here is what your fleet will need next year, here is the risk if you skip it, here is the evidence behind both.

Planned: 620 injections over 8 weeks, high salt buffer, 90% of max pressure → pump seal life consumed 71% replacement due week 6 → column life consumed 88% one column required by week 7 → lamp hours consumed 340 h no action this quarter → probability the campaign completes without intervention 82%
A twin that forecasts wear is a twin that forecasts parts. One subscription protects the customer's uptime and predicts the maker's revenue.
Where we are today

What is built, honestly.

Instrumented hardware twin
Live sensor node on a real motor, streaming vibration, temperature and current. Faults induced on demand and predicted before they are induced.
3D instrument simulator
HPLC and analyser models with a physics-driven state, so faults can be explored on instruments we cannot physically access.
Version compatibility sandbox
Fleet replay, per-instrument verdicts, version pinning, validation report and a vendor revert request with the failing evidence attached.
Time machine and scoreboard
Ninety days replayable per instrument, and a public record of every prediction with its outcome, including the misses.
The monitoring layer underneath
Health scores, alerts, WhatsApp tickets, NFC instrument records, complaint handling and audit trail — the original product, still the foundation.
Reports at the end of every path
Each simulation, validation and prediction ends in a document a lab can file, attach to a purchase request, or show in an audit.
What is not real yet, said plainly. The golden sets and the ninety day histories in these demos are synthetic. The engine, the comparisons and the reports are real and running. What turns this from a working prototype into a validated product is one thing we cannot generate ourselves: archived runs and service history from a real instrument family. That is our ask, and it is a small one.
